Content Audit Checklist for Pakistani SMEs

Published: November 23, 2026

By Abu Qitmir Mohammad Shiraz Al-Madani (AbuQitmirLabs)

Decide Which Pages to Keep, Update, Merge or Remove

Your quietest service page may be the one your sales team shares most.

A content audit helps a small business decide which website pages need attention before it spends money creating new ones. It is not only a traffic report. It checks whether each page is accurate, useful to buyers, easy to find and connected to a working next step.

For example, if customers keep asking on WhatsApp whether you serve their city or what your fee includes, check whether the website answers those questions clearly.

The simple rule is:

> Give every important URL one decision: keep, update, merge or review for removal.

Do not delete a page only because it has low traffic. First check how customers, sales staff and existing users rely on it.

At a Glance

  • Start with one core service and its connected pages (service page, pricing, case study, and contact path) rather than auditing the entire website at once.
  • Score every URL from 0 to 10 across five buyer criteria: accuracy, buyer clarity, proof, next action, and confirmed business use.
  • Assign one clear decision per URL: Keep (8-10), Update (5-7), Merge (3-4), or Review for removal (0-2) after verifying sales and customer usage.
  • Check Pakistan-specific signals: city coverage (Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad), working mobile WhatsApp links, PKR pricing clarity, and verifiable company details.

Quick checklist

Before you start, make sure you can answer these questions:

  • What does this page help a buyer do?
  • Is the information still accurate?
  • Does the page clearly explain who the service is for?
  • Do customers or sales staff still use the URL?
  • What evidence do we have from search, analytics or sales?
  • Is the next action clear on a mobile phone?
  • Does the page link to the next useful page?
  • Who owns the fix?
  • How will we check that the fix worked?

If you cannot confirm an answer, write unknown. Do not treat missing data as zero.

What a content audit checks

A content audit checks the page itself and the job it is meant to do. It asks whether the page answers a real customer question and makes the next step clear.

A traffic report only shows how often people reached the page through channels you can measure. A page with little search traffic may still be valuable if your sales team sends it to prospects before a call.

For example:

  • A service page answers: “What can I hire you to do?”
  • A pricing page answers: “What does the quote include?”
  • A case study answers: “What did you do for a customer?”
  • A location page answers: “Do you serve my city?”
  • A contact page answers: “How can I speak to you?”
  • A guide answers: “What should I know before I choose this service?”

Two pages can cover the same subject without being duplicates if they serve different questions or audiences. A service page and a case study may both mention the same service, but they do different jobs.

Five things to check on every page

1. Accuracy

Are the services, prices, locations, team details, delivery times and contact details still correct? Ask the person responsible for the service to confirm the facts.

2. Clarity

Can a first-time visitor understand the offer without knowing your business already? Remove vague phrases such as “complete solutions” unless you explain exactly what is included.

3. Proof

Does the page show a relevant example, result, process, testimonial, case study or explanation of how the work is delivered? Proof should support the specific claim being made.

For example, a software service page can link to the TajweedPage case study if the case study genuinely demonstrates the relevant engineering capability.

4. Next action

Does the reader know what to do next? The action might be to request a quote, send a WhatsApp message, view a case study or read a related guide. Test the button and form on a phone.

5. Internal links

Does the page help visitors move to the next useful page? A service page can link to a case study, pricing guide, FAQ, contact page or related service. Do not add links only for SEO. Add them where they help the reader make a decision.

Content audit vs technical SEO audit

A content problem is usually about accuracy, clarity, proof or usefulness. For example, a service page may still list a package that the company no longer sells.

A technical SEO problem affects access or delivery. Examples include broken links, incorrect redirects, slow loading, unwanted index settings or a form that does not work.

If you need to review page speed and technical signals, use the technical website audit tool. If customers keep asking the same questions, compare the page with what your team actually tells them.

A small company may need both audits. For example, rewriting an outdated service page will not help if the page is blocked from search or the enquiry form is broken.

Which pages should you audit first?

Do not begin with the oldest blog posts. Start with pages that affect revenue, trust or customer support.

Priority 1: Commercial pages

Review service pages, pricing pages, quote pages, product pages and contact pages first. These pages are closest to a buying decision.

Your custom software development page, for example, explains different software types, development stages, ownership and project scoping. A similar service page should answer the practical questions a buyer has before contacting you.

Priority 2: Trust pages

Review case studies, testimonials, team pages, About pages and security or process pages. Check whether they support the claims made on commercial pages.

A case study should explain the problem, the work completed and the outcome or current status. It should not be a collection of general marketing statements.

Priority 3: Acquisition pages

Review blog posts, guides, location pages and campaign landing pages. Ask whether they attract the right audience and send visitors to a relevant service page.

If a blog post receives visits but gives the reader no useful next step, add a relevant contextual link. For example, a guide about content and lead generation can link to the lead-generation content strategy.

Priority 4: Existing-user pages

Review resource pages, onboarding guides, checklists, event pages and documents that sales or customer support shares directly. These pages may have low search traffic but high business value.

Start with one service, not the whole website

A small business does not need to audit every URL on the first day. Start with one important service and collect the pages connected to it:

  • the main service page;
  • the pricing or quote page;
  • relevant guides;
  • case studies or examples;
  • the contact or enquiry page;
  • location or service-area pages; and
  • any page that sales regularly shares.

This small batch is easier to review and easier to improve. You can then use the same process for the rest of the website.

If the offer itself is unclear, review positioning before rewriting dozens of pages. The brand audit checklist for Pakistan can help you check whether the business promise, audience and positioning are clear.

If the problem is inconsistent wording or visuals across the website and social channels, use the brand consistency checklist. If you are building a broader identity system, see the guide on brand guidelines for small businesses in Pakistan.

If the problem is your visibility in Google Maps, review the listing separately. A local SEO audit can check the Google Business Profile, category, service-area settings and local signals. That is different from checking whether your website explains where you work.

A simple page-quality score

A score can help you prioritise work, but it should not replace judgement. Give each page a score from 0 to 2 in five areas:

Area0 points1 point2 points
AccuracyImportant facts are wrong or missingSome facts need checkingFacts are current and verified
Buyer clarityThe offer is difficult to understandThe offer is partly clearThe audience, problem and offer are clear
ProofNo useful evidenceGeneral claims or weak proofRelevant examples, results or process evidence
Next actionNo clear next step or broken actionAction exists but is unclear or difficultAction is clear and tested
Business useNobody can confirm a useOccasional or uncertain useSales, customers or search clearly use the page

Use the total only as a starting point:

  • 8–10: Keep and review later.
  • 5–7: Update and assign an owner.
  • 3–4: Compare with related pages before merging or rewriting.
  • 0–2: Review for removal, but check links, records and existing users first.

A low score does not automatically mean “delete.” It may mean that the page needs a clear purpose and a proper owner.

A newer publication date does not fix outdated information. The facts and offer must also be checked.

How to improve internal links

Internal links help visitors and search engines understand how your pages relate to one another. They also give a useful next step to people who are not ready to contact you yet.

Link from broad pages to specific pages

A general guide should link to the specific service that solves the problem. A guide about software operations can link to custom software engineering. A guide about AI workflows can link to AI agent development.

Link from service pages to proof

Every important service page should link to at least one relevant case study, example or process explanation. Avoid linking to a case study that does not support the service claim.

Link from blog posts to commercial pages

A blog post should not force every visitor to contact you immediately. Link first to the most useful next page, then offer the contact option. For example, a post about brand problems can link to the brand audit checklist before linking to the contact page.

Use descriptive anchor text

“Read more” is weak anchor text. Use words that describe the destination:

  • technical website audit;
  • local SEO audit;
  • brand consistency checklist;
  • custom software development; or
  • discuss a scoped content audit.

Avoid link stuffing

Do not add ten unrelated links to one paragraph. A useful rule is to add a link when it answers a question, proves a claim or provides the next logical step.

A simple worksheet

Create one row for every important URL. Include pages that sales shares even if they are not linked in the main blog menu.

How to audit your website in six steps

Step 1: Choose a question to test

Start with a question that matters to buyers. For example:

> Can a buyer understand our training formats and request the right one?

Choose a service where sales can describe common questions and the service owner can confirm the answers.

Step 2: Build your URL list

Collect URLs from your website editor, XML sitemap, sales team and analytics reports. A sitemap helps search engines discover pages, but it does not guarantee that they will crawl or index every page.

Look for URL variations. A campaign link and a plain URL may open the same page. Confirm the canonical URL before combining their data.

Also record URLs that are not visible in your main navigation. Sales may share them directly through WhatsApp, email or social media.

Step 3: Record evidence

For each page, record:

  • the date range for your data;
  • clicks and impressions, when available;
  • the last date when the facts were checked;
  • how sales or customers use the URL;
  • the current next action;
  • useful internal links; and
  • anything you could not verify.

Use the Google Search Console Performance report to review clicks, impressions and queries. Use the country filter if Pakistan is an important market.

The Google Analytics 4 landing page report shows the first page of a session. It does not prove that a person became a lead. A WhatsApp tap is not the same as a received message, and a form start is not a form submission.

Ask sales which links they send to buyers, but do not copy customer names or private conversation transcripts into the audit sheet.

Step 4: Choose one decision

Select keep, update, merge or review for removal. If the evidence is incomplete, leave the page in review instead of making a rushed decision.

Step 5: Assign the work

The service owner should approve the offer and facts. The editor should update the copy and internal links. A developer should handle redirects, removed URLs, forms and indexing changes.

When one page permanently replaces another, use a relevant destination and a server-side 301 or 308 redirect when appropriate. Do not redirect every old URL to an unrelated homepage. See Google's redirect guidance and URL-mapping guidance.

If a page is genuinely gone and has no suitable replacement, return 404 or 410 instead of showing an error page with a 200 success status. See Google's HTTP status guidance.

If the findings require a full rewrite, treat it as a separate work item. See technical content writing and SEO copywriting for the implementation stage.

Step 6: Check the result

After the change goes live:

  1. Open the new or updated page on a phone.
  2. Test the form, WhatsApp link or contact button.
  3. Check the redirect destination.
  4. Update internal links that still point to the old URL.
  5. Confirm that important pages are still reachable from the site.
  6. Compare results with a similar period.
  7. Record whether buyers understand the offer more clearly.

Keep buyer understanding separate from changes in search traffic. Search performance can change because of seasonality, campaigns, competitors or tracking changes.

Pakistan-specific checks for SME websites

A content audit for a Pakistani SME should include a few checks that are easy to miss in a generic checklist.

Location and service area

If you serve Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad, Rawalpindi or other cities, say so clearly. Explain whether delivery is on-site, remote or available only in selected areas. Do not create many near-identical city pages unless each page has genuinely useful local information.

WhatsApp and phone actions

Check whether the phone number is current and whether the WhatsApp link opens correctly on mobile. Make the expected response clear if the business does not reply immediately.

Language and terminology

Decide whether the page should be in English, Urdu or a bilingual format. Keep important service names consistent across the website, Google Business Profile and social accounts. Do not translate technical or legal terms in a way that changes their meaning.

Pricing and currency

If you show prices, use PKR where appropriate and state what is included. If the final price depends on scope, explain what changes the quote instead of displaying a misleading fixed number.

Trust and verification

Make it easy for a buyer to verify who operates the business. Keep the About page, contact details, company information and service claims consistent. Link to relevant work instead of making unsupported claims.

For businesses serving international clients, a useful market-specific guide such as Pakistan offshore development for GCC companies should clearly explain the audience, delivery model and next step.

Five mistakes to avoid

  1. Deleting pages because they have few clicks. Check whether sales, customers or existing users still rely on them.
  2. Treating missing data as zero. Write “unknown” when tracking cannot answer the question.
  3. Merging pages because their titles look similar. Compare the buyer, purpose, scope and next action.
  4. Adding internal links without context. A link should help the reader, prove a claim or provide the next useful step.
  5. Using `noindex` as access control. noindex controls search visibility, not access. Google must be able to crawl the page to see the directive. Read Google's `noindex` guidance.
  6. Calling the audit finished when the spreadsheet is complete. The work is finished only after the changes are published and tested.

How often should you repeat a content audit?

There is no single schedule for every website. Use a practical rhythm:

  • Review commercial pages whenever the offer, price, location or delivery process changes.
  • Review important service and contact pages every three to six months.
  • Review high-value guides at least twice a year.
  • Review event, campaign and temporary pages when the activity ends.
  • Run a small audit after a redesign, CMS migration or major tracking change.

The goal is not to update every page on the same day. The goal is to prevent important pages from becoming inaccurate or disconnected from the business.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a website content audit cost in Pakistan?

The cost depends on the number of URLs, the quality of the available data and whether the quote includes rewriting or technical work. Ask for an itemised PKR quote. Keep audit recommendations, copywriting, development and post-launch checks as separate deliverables.

How long does a content audit take?

It depends on the number of pages and how quickly the people responsible can confirm the facts. Start with one service and use that batch to estimate the rest. Allow extra time for approvals, redirects and testing.

What is the difference between a content audit and a technical SEO audit?

A content audit checks whether a page is accurate, useful and clear. A technical SEO audit checks whether search engines and users can discover, access and understand the page. Use both when you plan to merge URLs, remove pages or redesign the site.

Who should run a content audit?

Give one person ownership of the worksheet. Marketing can manage the URL list, sales can confirm buyer questions, service owners can approve the offer, and a developer can handle redirects, forms and indexing changes.

Should I delete pages with low traffic?

Not automatically. First check sales use, customer use, internal links, accuracy and possible replacements. Remove a page only when it has no remaining purpose and the responsible people approve the decision.

Can I start without Google Search Console?

Yes. Begin with your CMS, sitemap, sales links and a manual review of the pages. Mark search visibility as unknown. Add Search Console data later instead of guessing.

Start with the pages customers already use

A useful content audit does not begin with a large spreadsheet. It begins with one important service and the pages that help customers understand, compare or enquire about it.

Keep useful pages. Update weak answers. Merge genuine duplicates. Put uncertain removals through review. Add internal links that guide visitors to the next useful page.
