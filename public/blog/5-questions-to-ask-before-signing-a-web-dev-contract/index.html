5 Questions to Ask Before Signing a Web Dev Contract

Summary Box

> Summary: This guide outlines five specific questions buyers should ask any web development agency before signing a contract, covering code ownership, domain and hosting control, post-launch support commitments, response time guarantees, and what happens if the relationship ends — the exact details most agencies leave out of their pitch.

Quote

> "A contract that only describes what gets built and says nothing about what happens after isn't a contract — it's an invoice with extra steps."

Key highlights

  • Code, domain, and hosting ownership should be explicitly stated in writing — not assumed
  • "Post-launch support" without a defined timeframe or response time is not a real commitment
  • Asking who owns your domain registration is the single most important question most buyers skip
  • A legitimate agency can describe their handoff process without hesitation or vague language
  • Response time guarantees separate professional agencies from one-person freelance operations
  • The right time to ask these questions is before payment — not after something breaks

Introduction

You've narrowed it down to a few web development agencies. The portfolios look solid. The pricing seems fair. Someone on the team responds quickly to your messages. Everything about the pitch feels right.

Here's what almost nobody checks before signing: what happens after launch, who actually owns the domain and hosting account, and what your options are if the relationship doesn't work out six months from now.

These aren't paranoid questions. They're the exact details that separate a transparent, professional outsourcing partner from a setup where you discover — usually at the worst possible moment — that you don't actually control your own website.

This isn't about distrusting every agency you talk to. It's about asking five specific questions before any money changes hands, because the agencies doing things right will answer immediately, and the ones that aren't will hesitate in ways you'll learn to recognize.

What "Signing a Website Development Contract" Actually Involves

A website development contract isn't just a price and a delivery date. A properly written one defines four separate things: what gets built, who owns it once it's built, what support exists after launch, and what happens if either party wants to end the relationship.

Most contracts buyers receive only cover the first one. The price, the page count, the timeline. The other three — ownership, support, and exit terms — are either missing entirely or buried in language vague enough that nobody notices until it matters.

This gap is exactly where most post-launch problems start. Not because agencies are dishonest, but because an unwritten assumption ("of course we'll help you after launch," "of course you own the domain") isn't worth anything once a disagreement happens.

> "If a commitment isn't in the contract, it isn't a commitment. It's a hope."

Why This Matters More in 2026 Than It Used To

The outsourcing market has grown fast — more agencies, more freelancers, more remote teams competing for the same projects. That's good for buyers in terms of pricing, but it also means more variation in how professionally contracts are handled.

Ownership disputes have become more visible. As businesses increasingly run their actual sales and customer relationships through their website, losing access to a domain or hosting account isn't a minor inconvenience anymore — it can stop revenue entirely for days or weeks.

AI-assisted development has changed who can call themselves a "developer." With AI tools accelerating how quickly a site can be assembled, the barrier to entry for offering web development services has dropped. That's not inherently bad, but it means contract discipline matters more, not less, when evaluating who you're hiring.

Remote and cross-border outsourcing is now the default, not the exception. When your developer is in a different country or time zone, verbal assurances carry even less practical weight — written terms are what you can actually act on if something goes wrong.

Common Mistakes People Make Before Signing

  1. Assuming "we'll take care of you" means anything specific. Friendly language during a sales call isn't a contractual commitment — and most disputes happen exactly where the friendly language and the actual contract didn't match.
  2. Never asking who registers the domain. Many buyers don't realize their agency registered the domain under the agency's own account until they try to switch providers and discover they need permission to leave.
  3. Treating "support included" as self-explanatory. Support could mean anything from 24-hour bug fixes to a single follow-up email a month after launch. Without specifics, it means whatever the agency decides it means later.
  4. Not asking what tech stack or platform the site is built on. This matters for ownership too — a site built on a developer's private, proprietary system can be effectively impossible to move elsewhere.
  5. Signing before getting anything in writing about the handoff process. Verbal promises about login access and documentation evaporate the moment communication slows down.

The 5 Questions — Step-by-Step Guide

Here is exactly what to ask, and what a real answer should sound like.

1. "Who will own the domain, hosting account, and source code once the project is complete?"

What a good answer sounds like: "The domain gets registered under your name and your account from day one. Hosting is set up under your credentials, or transferred to you immediately at launch. You receive full source code and repository access on final payment."

What a red flag answer sounds like: Vague reassurance without specifics — "Don't worry, we'll take care of all that for you" — without naming who actually holds the accounts.

This is the single most important question on this list. If you don't own your domain registration, you do not fully own your website, regardless of what anyone tells you.

2. "What exactly is included in post-launch support, and for how long?"

What a good answer sounds like: A specific number of days or weeks of included bug fixes after launch, a stated response time (e.g., "within 24-48 hours for critical issues"), and a clear explanation of what happens after that period — usually a maintenance retainer with defined pricing.

What a red flag answer sounds like: "We're always here if you need anything" with no defined scope, timeframe, or what happens if you need help eight months later.

3. "What platform or technology is this website built on, and can I move it elsewhere if I need to?"

What a good answer sounds like: A specific, nameable technology (WordPress, Shopify, a custom React build, etc.) along with confirmation that the codebase is portable and not locked to a proprietary system only that agency can maintain.

What a red flag answer sounds like: Avoiding the question, or explaining that the site runs on "our own system" that only their team can edit or host.

4. "What is your average response time for support requests, and is that written into the contract?"

What a good answer sounds like: A specific number, ideally tiered by urgency (critical security issue vs. minor content update), and confirmation that this is documented — not just stated verbally.

What a red flag answer sounds like: "We're usually pretty quick" with no number attached, and no mention of putting it in writing.

5. "What happens if I want to end the relationship — do I get full access to everything immediately?"

What a good answer sounds like: Immediate transfer of all logins, source files, and documentation upon request, regardless of the reason for ending the relationship, as long as payment terms are settled.

What a red flag answer sounds like: Conditions, delays, or hesitation tied to anything other than outstanding payment — this is the clearest signal of a relationship designed to be hard to leave.

What a Transparent Web Development Contract Actually Looks Like

A contract that protects both sides typically includes these elements, laid out clearly rather than buried in legal language:

  1. Scope of work — exactly what is being built, in specific terms, not vague descriptions
  2. Ownership clause — explicit statement that domain, hosting, and code transfer to the client
  3. Support period — a defined number of days/weeks of included post-launch support
  4. Response time commitments — specific hours, tiered by issue severity
  5. Maintenance terms beyond the support period — pricing and scope for ongoing care, if the client wants it
  6. Exit terms — what happens, and how quickly, if either party ends the engagement

> "The agencies most confident in their work are also the most willing to put every one of these terms in writing — because they have nothing to hide and no interest in making it hard for you to leave."

Realistic Example

A small retail business in Karachi signed with a freelancer for a new e-commerce website. The price was reasonable, the timeline was short, and the freelancer seemed responsive throughout the build. Nowhere in the agreement was it specified who would own the domain registration — the freelancer had simply registered it during setup, "to make things easier."

Eight months later, the business wanted to switch to a different developer to add features the original site couldn't support. The freelancer became unresponsive. Without ownership of the domain, the business couldn't point it to a new host without the freelancer's cooperation — cooperation that took nearly three weeks of back-and-forth to finally get.

The fix going forward was simple: before signing with the next developer, the business owner asked all five questions in this article directly, in writing, before any deposit was paid. The new agency answered each one specifically and registered the domain under the business's own account from the first day of the project. The website cost about the same as the first one. The difference was control — and that control only existed because the questions were asked before the contract was signed, not after a problem appeared. How a Properly Outsourced Website Development Process Should Handle This

When outsourcing website development — whether to a local agency, a freelancer, or an international remote team — these five answers should be available before any contract is finalized, not negotiated after a problem surfaces:

  • Ownership terms confirmed in writing, not implied through friendly conversation
  • A documented support window with specific dates, not an open-ended verbal promise
  • Named technology stack, so you always know what your site is built on and whether it's portable
  • Tiered response time commitments, especially for anything affecting site uptime or security
  • Clear, unconditional exit terms, separate from any payment disputes

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the most important question to ask before signing a website contract?

Who owns the domain registration. This single detail determines whether you can leave an agency or developer freely in the future, regardless of any other terms in the contract.

Is it normal for an agency to register my domain under their own account?

It happens often, but it shouldn't be the default. A transparent agency will register the domain under your account and email from the very beginning, or transfer it to you immediately at launch with no conditions.

How much post-launch support is reasonable to expect?

Most professional agencies include 30 to 90 days of included bug-fixing support after launch, with a separate paid maintenance option afterward. Anything described only as "ongoing support" without a defined period should be clarified before signing.

What should I do if an agency refuses to answer these questions clearly?

Treat it as useful information. An agency that hesitates on ownership, support scope, or response times before you've paid anything is unlikely to become more transparent after you have.

Can I add these terms to a contract an agency already sent me?

Yes. These are reasonable, standard requests. Asking an agency to add explicit ownership and support language to their contract is a normal part of negotiation, not an unusual demand.

Does this apply to small projects too, or only large enterprise contracts?

It applies at every budget level. Domain ownership disputes and unclear support terms happen just as often — sometimes more often — on smaller projects where contracts are treated less formally.

Final Thoughts

None of this is about assuming the worst of every developer or agency you talk to. Most people in this industry do good work and have no interest in trapping a client in a bad arrangement.

But good intentions on a sales call aren't the same as clear terms in a signed agreement. The five questions in this article take less than ten minutes to ask, and a legitimate agency will answer all of them without hesitation, specifics included.

If you get clear, written answers — you've likely found a team worth working with. If you get vague reassurance instead, you've learned something valuable before any money changed hands, which is exactly when you want to learn it.
