## Introduction: You Paid for It. But Do You Own It?

You paid $40,000 for your app. The developer delivered it. The invoice is settled. You assume you own the software.

In most jurisdictions, you do not.

That is not a technicality. It is the default rule in copyright law. The person who writes the code owns it unless there is a written agreement transferring ownership to you. Paying an invoice does not change that. Sending an email that says "work made for hire" does not change that.

What changes it is an **IP assignment clause** in a signed contract.

Founders discover this during fundraising, acquisition, or when they try to switch developers. An investor's lawyer asks for the IP assignment paperwork. There is none. The deal stalls while everyone scrambles to get a former contractor in another country to sign a document they have no incentive to sign.

This guide gives you a step-by-step checklist to prevent that scenario. It covers the 18 assets you must own, the contract clauses to look for, and the verification steps to run before signing anything.

## Why Most Founders Assume Ownership Transfers Automatically

Most founders are not lawyers. They assume that paying for something means owning it. That assumption works for physical goods. It does not work for intellectual property.

Copyright law treats software differently. The default rule is that the author owns the work. If you hire a contractor to write code, the contractor is the author. If you do not have a written assignment, the contractor owns the copyright to the software you paid them to build.

There is a narrow exception called "work made for hire." It applies mostly to employees, not independent contractors. Putting that phrase in an email or a proposal does not satisfy the legal requirements for a work-made-for-hire relationship. Only a properly drafted written agreement does.

The same logic applies to every asset connected to your product. Domains, repositories, cloud accounts, app store credentials, design files. If those are registered to personal accounts, you do not own them, no matter how much you paid.

## The 18-Step Software Ownership & IP Checklist

This checklist covers every asset you need to claim. Run through it before signing any development contract. If you have already signed, run through it now. It is never too late to fix, but it gets harder every day.

### Step 1: Verify Your IP Assignment Clause in Writing

Open your development contract and search for an intellectual property assignment clause. It must explicitly state that all work product, including source code, documentation, design files, and derivative works, is assigned to you or your company. If the clause says "work made for hire," check that it includes the specific language required in your jurisdiction. If there is no IP assignment clause at all, do not sign until one is added.

### Step 2: Confirm the Code Repository Is in Your Name

The code repository is the most important asset you will ever own for a software product. It must be registered under your name or your company entity. The developer should be added as a collaborator or admin, not the owner. Check GitHub, GitLab, or Bitbucket and confirm your role says "owner" or "admin." If the repository lives in a personal account controlled by a developer, you do not control your product.

### Step 3: Confirm the Domain Name Is Registered to Your Company

The domain name is frequently the most misfiled asset. Check the registrar account. The registrant email should be yours. The billing should be in your name. The domain should not be registered to a developer's personal email, even if they set it up for you. Domain ownership is verified by ICANN lookup, not by who pays the annual fee.

### Step 4: Confirm Cloud Hosting and Infrastructure Accounts Are in Your Name

Your application runs on cloud infrastructure. That infrastructure must be billed to your company and accessed through accounts you control. Check AWS, Google Cloud, Vercel, DigitalOcean, or whichever platform hosts your product. The billing page should show your payment method. The root account should be yours.

### Step 5: Confirm App Store Developer Accounts Are Registered to Your Business Entity

If your product has mobile apps, the Apple Developer Program membership and Google Play Console account must be registered to your legal entity. Moving an app between developer accounts later is possible, but it is tedious and it interrupts your users. Verify this before the app ships, not after.

### Step 6: Confirm Design Source Files Are Accessible and Editable

Design source files (Figma, Sketch, Adobe XD) are not the same as exported images. Exported images cannot be edited. Source files can. Confirm you have edit access to the original files, not just view-only links. This matters the first time you need to update the interface or onboard a new designer.

### Step 7: Confirm All API Credentials and Third-Party Service Logins Are Yours

List every third-party service connected to your product: payment processors, email providers, SMS gateways, analytics platforms, CRM integrations, and any other API. Confirm you have owner access to each one. This includes the ability to rotate keys, change billing, and revoke developer access. A developer who holds your Stripe API key holds your revenue.

### Step 8: Confirm Analytics and Data Export Access

You should have admin access to your analytics dashboards and the ability to export your full database. Test this by running an export. Confirm you can read the result. Data is not just a technical asset. It is your customer list, your usage patterns, and your business intelligence.

### Step 9: Confirm Documentation Is Complete and Accessible

Technical documentation, architecture notes, environment setup instructions, and API documentation should be in a repository you control. If the only person who understands how the system works is the developer who built it, you have a dependency problem. Documentation is the bridge between your current team and whatever team comes next.

### Step 10: Confirm the Contract Defines Deliverables Explicitly

Your contract should define deliverables by name. It should list the source code specifically, not just "the application." It should list documentation, design files, and any other tangible outputs. Vague deliverable definitions create disputes later.

### Step 11: Confirm the Contract Addresses Late Payment and Transfer Timing

Many contracts hold IP transfer until the final invoice clears. That is a fair and common arrangement. But you should know about it before you sign. If the contract says ownership transfers only upon full payment, plan accordingly. Do not release final payment until you have verified every asset.

### Step 12: Confirm the Contract Includes a Confidentiality Clause

Your idea, your data, your customers, and your product roadmap should all be covered by confidentiality. This is not just about protecting against theft. It is about establishing the legal foundation for trade secret protection. If the developer discloses your architecture or your customer list, you have no recourse without a confidentiality clause.

### Step 13: Confirm the Contract Includes Termination and Handoff Terms

What happens if the relationship ends? The contract should spell out exactly what you receive: a full copy of the repository, documentation, credentials, and a reasonable transition period. If the contract is silent on this, ask for it before you sign. It costs nothing at that stage and it is awkward to negotiate later.

### Step 14: Confirm No Developer Holds a Personal Account with Your Assets

Audit every account connected to your product. If a developer created an account in their personal name for any service your product depends on, that is a red flag. The account should be transferred or recreated under your company entity. Do not accept "it's fine, I'll transfer it later."

### Step 15: Confirm Open Source Components Are Documented and Compliant

Your product almost certainly uses open source code. That is fine. What matters is that you know which licenses apply and whether any of them impose obligations on your distribution model. A copyleft license (like GPL) can require you to release your own source code if you distribute the software. Run a license scan or ask your developer for a software bill of materials.

### Step 16: Confirm No Founder Has a Prior Employer IP Conflict

If any founder worked on the product while employed elsewhere, review their employment agreement. Many employers assert ownership over IP created during employment, even if it was unrelated to the job and produced outside work hours. The best way to head off this issue is to identify exactly what rights the employer might assert. If there is overlap, consider waiting a designated period before working on the startup.

### Step 17: Confirm All Contributors Have Signed IP Assignments

It is not just the development agency. Every founder, every employee, every contractor, every advisor who touches the product should sign an IP assignment. This includes the designer who made your logo, the copywriter who wrote your landing page, and the intern who fixed a CSS bug. If you cannot produce a signed assignment for every contributor, you do not have a clean chain of title.

### Step 18: Verify Ownership by Logging In Yourself

The final step is verification. Log into every asset yourself. Do not take anyone's word for it. Open the repository. Confirm your role. Open the registrar. Confirm the registrant email. Open the cloud console. Confirm the billing page. Run the checklist in your browser, not in your imagination.

## Red Flags That Should Concern You

**Red Flag 1: The developer owns the repository.** If your code lives in the developer's personal GitHub account, you do not control your product. This is the single most common ownership problem we see.

**Red Flag 2: The domain is registered to a developer's email.** Domain ownership is verified by the registrant email, not by who pays the fee. If the developer holds the registration, they control the domain.

**Red Flag 3: The contract has no IP assignment clause.** Do not sign. This is not negotiable. Without an assignment, you may only have an implied license to use the software you paid for.

**Red Flag 4: The developer refuses to share access to accounts.** Legitimate confidentiality concerns can exist. But a developer who refuses all reasonable verification is a developer who may be holding your assets hostage.

**Red Flag 5: "Work made for hire" appears in an email, not a contract.** That phrase in an email does not transfer ownership. It is not a legal document. Only a signed agreement does.

**Red Flag 6: Your product uses GPL or AGPL code without a commercial license.** Copyleft licenses can require you to release your own source code. If you are building a proprietary product, this is a serious problem.

**Red Flag 7: No contributor has signed an IP assignment.** Every person who touches your product must sign one. Missing assignments create gaps in your chain of title that investors will find.

## Common Mistakes Founders Make

**Mistake 1: Assuming payment equals ownership.** It does not. The default rule in copyright law is that the author owns the work. You need a written assignment.

**Mistake 2: Trusting an email instead of a contract.** "Work made for hire" in an email is not a legal transfer. It must be in a signed agreement with explicit assignment language.

**Mistake 3: Letting the developer own the repository "for convenience."** Convenience now creates six-figure problems later. The repository should be in your name from the first commit.

**Mistake 4: Forgetting the domain name.** It is the most commonly misfiled asset. Check it first.

**Mistake 5: Skipping the verification step.** Do not take anyone's word for it. Log into every account yourself and confirm your role.

**Mistake 6: Assuming the contract covers everything.** Read the contract. If it does not mention IP assignment, deliverables, or handoff, it does not cover what matters.

**Mistake 7: Waiting until fundraising to fix ownership problems.** Fixing ownership during diligence is expensive, slow, and sometimes impossible. Fix it now.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who owns the source code when I hire a developer?

In most jurisdictions, the person who writes the code owns the copyright by default unless there is a written agreement transferring ownership. Paying for development does not automatically transfer IP rights. You need a written IP assignment clause in your contract.

### What is an IP assignment agreement?

An IP assignment agreement is a legal document that transfers ownership of intellectual property from the creator to another party. Every founder, employee, and contractor who works on your product should sign one before touching any sensitive material.

### What digital assets should a founder own?

Source code repositories, domain names, cloud hosting accounts, app store developer accounts, design source files, API credentials, third-party service logins, analytics access, and documentation. Each asset should be registered to the company entity.

### What happens if the IP assignment is missing from the contract?

Without an IP assignment, you may only have an implied license to use the software. You may not be able to transfer it, sell it, or hand it to a new development team. This becomes critical during fundraising or acquisition.

### Should the code repository be in my name or the developer's?

The repository should be owned by you or your company entity. The developer should be added as a collaborator, not the owner. Access can be revoked, but ownership cannot be easily taken back.

### What is a software escrow?

A software escrow deposits source code with a neutral third party. If the developer goes out of business, the escrow releases code to the client. It is a risk mitigation tool, not a substitute for IP ownership.

### How do I verify I own my software?

Log into every asset yourself. Confirm your role says owner or admin. Check that billing and email addresses are yours. Review contracts for explicit IP assignment language.
