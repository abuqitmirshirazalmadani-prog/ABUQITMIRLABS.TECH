## Introduction

Three weeks ago, your web developer answered every message within the hour. Progress updates came in regularly. Then, somewhere around milestone three, the replies started slowing down. A day became three days. Three days became silence.

Now you're sitting with a half-finished website, a chunk of money already paid, and a person who simply isn't responding anymore.

This happens more often than most people realize, and it happens at every price point — to businesses working with $200 freelancers and businesses working with $20,000 agencies alike. It's rarely something you did wrong. Sometimes it's overcommitment on their end, sometimes it's a payment disagreement neither side handled well, and sometimes it really is someone who took a deposit and vanished.

Whatever the reason, panic doesn't help right now. What helps is a clear sequence of steps, taken in order, starting with the thing most people skip first.

---

## Why This Happens (And Why It's Usually Not Personal)

Before jumping into recovery steps, it helps to understand the most common reasons a developer goes quiet — because the reason often determines which recovery path will actually work.

**Overcommitment.** Many freelancers and small agencies take on more projects than they can realistically manage. When deadlines start slipping across multiple clients at once, communication is often the first thing to break down — not because the work stopped, but because facing the backlog feels worse than avoiding it.

**A scope or payment disagreement that was never resolved out loud.** If a milestone payment was late, or the client asked for changes the developer felt were outside the original agreement, some people go quiet rather than have an uncomfortable conversation about it.

**Genuine personal or business disruption.** Illness, a closed business, a freelancer moving on to full-time employment — these happen, and they're rarely communicated well, if at all.

**Outright abandonment after a deposit.** This is the least common reason but the one people fear most. It does happen, and the steps below work whether you're dealing with someone overwhelmed or someone who never intended to finish the work.

> "Knowing which of these you're dealing with changes your approach, but the first three steps are the same regardless."

---

## Common Mistakes People Make When This Happens

1. **Sending angry or emotional messages first.** It's understandable, but a hostile message is the easiest one to ignore, and it gives an unresponsive developer a convenient excuse to stay unresponsive.

2. **Waiting too long before taking action.** Many business owners wait six, eight, even twelve weeks "just in case they come back," losing valuable time that could have gone toward documentation or recovery.

3. **Not checking what access they actually have first.** Some business owners assume the worst — that they've lost everything — without confirming what they genuinely have versus what's actually missing.

4. **Threatening legal action immediately.** This usually escalates things without giving either side a real chance to resolve it simply, and it can make a cooperative resolution harder to reach.

5. **Forgetting that payment platforms exist as a recovery option.** Many people don't realize PayPal, bank cards, and similar platforms have formal dispute processes that exist for exactly this kind of situation.

## What to Do — Step by Step

### Step 1: Document Everything Immediately

Before sending another message or taking any other action, gather everything you have in one place. Do this even if you're hoping the developer responds tomorrow.

- Screenshot or export every message thread (email, WhatsApp, Slack, whatever platform you used)
- Save copies of every invoice, payment receipt, and bank or PayPal transaction record
- List every deliverable received so far — files, designs, staging links, login credentials
- Note the dates of the last few exchanges and exactly what was promised in each

This isn't about building a legal case yet. It's about making sure nothing disappears before you need it, and giving yourself a clear picture of exactly where things stand.

### Step 2: Check What You Actually Have Access To

This step often changes the entire situation. Many business owners assume they've lost everything, when in reality they have more control than they think.

Check each of the following:

- **Domain registrar.** Log in to where the domain was purchased (GoDaddy, Namecheap, etc.). Is it under your own account, or was it registered under the developer's account?
- **Hosting account.** Same question — do you have your own login, or is hosting under their account?
- **Website backend/CMS.** Can you log into WordPress, Shopify admin, or whatever platform was used?
- **Source code.** Was anything shared via GitHub, GitLab, or a shared drive folder?
- **Design files.** Figma, Adobe XD, or similar — do you have viewer or editor access?

> "If you have your own domain and hosting login, you're in a far better position than you think — even with an unfinished website."

### Step 3: Recovering Domain and Hosting Access (If You Don't Have It)

If the domain or hosting is under the developer's account, this is the priority to resolve first, because it determines whether your business has any online presence at all going forward.

1. **Contact the domain registrar directly**, not just the developer. Most registrars (GoDaddy, Namecheap, Google Domains successors, etc.) have a process for proving you are the rightful business owner of a domain, even when it's registered under someone else's account. You'll typically need your business registration documents, the original purchase receipt if you have it, and proof of the business the domain represents.

2. **Contact the hosting provider** the same way. Hosting companies generally have account recovery and dispute processes, especially when you can show you paid for the service or own the business the site represents.

3. **If you paid the developer directly for domain/hosting** (rather than registering it yourself), this becomes part of your payment dispute — see Step 4.

This process typically takes anywhere from a few days to a few weeks depending on the registrar and how much documentation you can provide.

### Step 4: When and How to Escalate

If steps 1 through 3 haven't resolved things and you still have no response, escalation is reasonable. Do it in this order:

1. **Send one final, calm, written message** with a specific deadline. State clearly what you need (a status update, remaining deliverables, or a refund) and give a reasonable date — typically 5 to 7 business days. Keep the tone factual, not emotional. This message matters because it becomes part of your documentation if you need to dispute a payment later.

2. **If you paid by card or PayPal**, look into a chargeback or dispute. Most payment platforms have a formal process for "services not rendered" and a defined window (often 120-180 days) in which to file. This is one of the most underused recovery options, and it works in a meaningful percentage of cases.

3. **If the amount is significant and other options haven't worked**, consider whether small claims court (for local developers) or a formal demand letter makes sense for your situation. This is rarely necessary for smaller projects, but worth knowing as an option for larger ones.

> "A calm, dated, written request accomplishes more than three angry follow-up messages — and it's the document you'll want later if things don't resolve."

## Realistic Example

A small retail business owner hired a freelancer through a job platform to rebuild their online store. Communication was strong for the first month, then slowed dramatically after the second milestone payment. After three weeks of silence, the owner followed this exact sequence: documented every message and payment, checked their accounts, and discovered the domain was registered under their own business account from the start — the freelancer simply hadn't finished connecting the new hosting.

With domain ownership confirmed, the business contacted the hosting provider directly, explained the situation, and got temporary access within four days using their payment receipt as proof. They sent one final written message to the freelancer with a 7-day deadline for either resuming work or providing a partial refund. No response came. They filed a dispute through their payment provider for the unfinished milestone and recovered roughly 60% of what was still owed.

Total time from "went quiet" to having a working plan forward: about two weeks. The site wasn't finished by the original freelancer, but the business never lost its domain, and recovered enough of the payment to fund hiring someone else to complete it properly.

## Step 5: Hiring the Next Developer the Right Way

Once you've recovered what you can, the most important thing is making sure this doesn't happen again. Two things matter here, and both come down to information you can act on immediately.

**Ask the right questions before signing anything.** Before committing to a new developer or agency, ask directly who will own the domain and hosting, what's included in post-launch support and for how long, what technology the site will be built on, what the response time commitment is, and what happens if either side wants to end the relationship. A transparent developer answers all of this clearly and immediately.

**Get it in writing.** A simple contract that states domain ownership, hosting access, payment milestones, and support terms protects both sides — and it's the single biggest difference between this situation happening once and it happening repeatedly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I get my money back if my developer disappeared?
Often, partially. If you paid by card or PayPal, a formal dispute for "services not rendered" is your strongest option, typically available for 120 to 180 days after payment. If you paid in cash or bank transfer directly with no platform protection, recovery is harder but not impossible, especially with a documented written demand.

### What if I don't have the domain login?
Contact the domain registrar directly, not just the developer. Registrars have processes to verify business ownership of a domain even when the account belongs to someone else, usually requiring your business documents and any proof of payment you have.

### How long should I wait before assuming they've disappeared?
There's no universal rule, but if you've sent two follow-up messages over 10 to 14 days with zero response, it's reasonable to start the documentation and access-checking process even if you're still hoping they'll come back. Starting the process doesn't mean giving up on a resolution — it just means you're prepared either way.

### Is it normal for freelancers to go quiet sometimes and still finish the work?
Yes, this happens, particularly with independent freelancers juggling multiple projects. A short gap of a few days isn't unusual. The recovery steps in this guide are for situations extending well beyond that, where messages and deadlines have been consistently ignored.

### Should I report them somewhere?
If you hired through a freelance platform (Upwork, Fiverr, etc.), report the issue through their dispute resolution system — most platforms have built-in protections for exactly this scenario, including escrow holds that work in your favor. If you hired independently, a public, factual review (not an angry one) can help others, though it won't recover your funds directly.

### What's the single most important thing to check first?
Domain ownership. If you have control of your own domain registration, you have far more leverage and options than someone who doesn't — even if the website itself is unfinished.

## Final Thoughts

Going quiet is rarely about you personally, even when it feels that way. Most of the time it's overcommitment, an avoided conversation, or circumstances that have nothing to do with the quality of your project or how reasonable you were as a client.

What actually matters now is the sequence: document what you have, check what you control, recover what's recoverable, and escalate calmly if you need to. Most situations like this resolve — fully or partially — within a few weeks once someone takes these steps in order instead of waiting and hoping.

And once it's resolved, the best protection against this happening again isn't avoiding developers altogether. It's asking a few direct questions before you sign anything next time, and making sure the answers are written down.
