## Introduction: The $2,000 "Make It Pop" Mistake

You hire a freelance graphic designer. You send them a message saying, "I need a logo and some social media graphics. Make it look modern and pop." 

Two weeks later, they send you a design. You hate it. You ask for revisions. They send another version. You still hate it. They get frustrated, you get frustrated, and you end up paying for a project you cannot use.

The problem is not the designer's skill. The problem is the brief.

A **design brief template** is not a bureaucratic formality. It is the strategic foundation of the entire project. When you brief a designer properly, you save time, save money on revision rounds, and get a brand asset that actually drives revenue. 

Here is the 12-point checklist every founder should use before hiring a designer.

## The 12-Point Design Brief Checklist

### 1. Project Overview & Background
**Direct Answer:** Start by explaining what your company does in one paragraph, as if you were explaining it to a stranger.

**The Why:** Designers need context. If you are a B2B SaaS company selling to enterprise CTOs, your design should look very different from a B2C food delivery app. 

**Actionable Tip:** Include your company website URL and a one-sentence elevator pitch in this section.

### 2. Target Audience (Who is this for?)
**Direct Answer:** Define exactly who will see this design and what their motivations are.

**The Why:** You are not designing for yourself; you are designing for your customer. A design that appeals to a Gen Z demographic will fail if your target audience is 50-year-old corporate executives. 

**Actionable Tip:** List demographics (age, location, gender) and psychographics (what they care about, what their pain points are).

### 3. Business Objectives (The "Why")
**Direct Answer:** State exactly what this design needs to achieve for your business.

**The Why:** Are you trying to generate leads? Increase brand awareness? Explain a complex product? The visual hierarchy of the design changes based on the objective. 

**Actionable Tip:** Use specific verbs. Instead of "make it look good," say "I need this landing page to convince users to book a demo."

### 4. Specific Deliverables (Formats & Sizes)
**Direct Answer:** List every single asset you need, including exact dimensions and file formats.

**The Why:** If you ask for a "social media graphic," the designer might give you a square. But if you need it for a LinkedIn banner, you need a rectangle. If you need it for print, you need CMYK and 300 DPI; if for web, RGB and 72 DPI. 

**Actionable Tip:** Create a bulleted list. 
- 1x Primary Logo (AI, SVG, PNG)
- 1x Instagram Carousel (1080x1080px)
- 1x LinkedIn Banner (1584x396px)

### 5. Budget & Timeline
**Direct Answer:** Be transparent about how much you can spend and exactly when you need the final files.

**The Why:** A designer's scope of work is directly tied to their price. A $500 budget will get you a clean, custom template. A $5,000 budget will get you a fully custom, multi-week brand identity sprint. Similarly, a 2-day deadline usually means a rush fee.

**Actionable Tip:** Give a range for the budget and a hard deadline for the final files, not just a vague "as soon as possible."

### 6. Brand Guidelines & Existing Assets
**Direct Answer:** Provide any existing brand rules, logos, fonts, or color palettes that must be followed.

**The Why:** If you are building on an existing brand, the designer cannot just invent new colors. They need your existing assets to maintain consistency. 

**Actionable Tip:** Link to a Google Drive or Figma folder with your existing assets. If you don't have any, state that this project is starting from scratch, as outlined in our guide on [brand identity vs logo design](https://www.abuqitmirlabs.tech/blog/brand-identity-vs-logo-design).

### 7. Style Preferences & Inspiration (Mood Board)
**Direct Answer:** Share 3 to 5 visual examples of designs you love, and 1 to 2 examples of designs you hate.

**The Why:** Words are subjective. "Modern" means one thing to you and a completely different thing to the designer. Visual references align expectations instantly.

**Actionable Tip:** Use Pinterest or a shared Figma board. Explain *why* you like each example (e.g., "I like the typography here" or "I like the color contrast here").

### 8. Competitor Analysis
**Direct Answer:** Share links to competitors and explain what you want to do differently.

**The Why:** This prevents accidental plagiarism. It also helps the designer understand the market landscape and how to make your brand stand out.

**Actionable Tip:** Provide 2-3 competitor URLs and note their weaknesses (e.g., "Their site feels cluttered; we want something minimal").

### 9. Messaging & Copy
**Direct Answer:** Provide the exact text that needs to go into the design.

**The Why:** Designers are not copywriters. If you ask them to write your headline, they will often use filler text (Lorem Ipsum) or write something generic. 

**Actionable Tip:** If you need help writing the copy for your design, review our [content writing](https://www.abuqitmirlabs.tech/content-writing) services or use a copywriter before briefing the designer.

### 10. Call to Action (CTA)
**Direct Answer:** Identify the single most important button or link in the design.

**The Why:** Every design has a job to do. If there are five buttons competing for attention, the user will click none of them. 

**Actionable Tip:** Specify the exact text for the CTA (e.g., "Download Now," "Book a Call") and ask the designer to make it the most visually prominent element.

### 11. Revision & Feedback Process
**Direct Answer:** Define exactly how many rounds of revisions are included and how feedback will be delivered.

**The Why:** "Endless revisions" is how designers lose money and founders lose patience. Professional design contracts usually include 2 or 3 rounds of revisions. 

**Actionable Tip:** Use a tool like Figma or Markup.io to leave comments directly on the design, rather than writing a long email with vague descriptions.

### 12. Success Metrics (How will you measure it?)
**Direct Answer:** Define what a successful design looks like in numbers.

**The Why:** A logo is hard to measure, but a landing page design is easy. By defining the metric upfront, you focus the designer on the outcome, not just the aesthetics.

**Actionable Tip:** If the design is for a [web development](https://www.abuqitmirlabs.tech/web-development) project, ask the designer to ensure the layout supports high conversion rates, as outlined in our [UI/UX design conversion](https://www.abuqitmirlabs.tech/blog/ui-ux-design-conversion) guide.

## Common Mistakes Founders Make

### Mistake 1: Asking for a Logo When You Need a Brand System
A logo is just a mark. If you do not brief the designer to create typography and color rules, you will pay twice. Use a [design system for startups](https://www.abuqitmirlabs.tech/blog/design-systems-for-startups) approach from day one.

### Mistake 2: Not Providing Real Copy
Using "Lorem Ipsum" (dummy text) in a design means the layout will break the moment you insert your actual, longer paragraphs. Provide real text upfront.

### Mistake 3: Design by Committee
If you ask five different co-founders for feedback, you will get five different opinions. Appoint one single decision-maker for the design project.

### Mistake 4: Forgetting Mobile
70% of web traffic is mobile. If you do not explicitly ask the designer to provide mobile layouts for a web design, you will get a desktop-only mockup.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a design brief?
A design brief is a written document that outlines the scope, objectives, target audience, deliverables, and constraints of a design project. It acts as a contract between the client and the designer to ensure alignment.

### What should be included in a graphic design brief?
A comprehensive brief includes the company background, target audience, business objectives, specific deliverables (formats and sizes), budget, timeline, brand guidelines, inspiration, and the exact copy to be used.

### How long should a design brief be?
A design brief does not need to be a 20-page document. A well-structured, 1-to-2-page document with bullet points and clear headings is usually sufficient for most projects.

### Do I need to know design terms to write a brief?
No. You just need to know your business and your customer. If you have visual references (like a Pinterest board), you can point to them to explain what you like without knowing the technical terms.

### Should I ask a designer for free work (spec work) before hiring them?
No. Spec work (asking multiple designers to design something for free before choosing one) is unethical and illegal in many places. Instead, review their existing portfolio to see if their style matches your brand.

### What is a mood board?
A mood board is a collection of images, colors, and typography samples that convey a specific visual direction. It helps align the designer's vision with the founder's expectations.

### How many revisions should I expect?
Standard practice is 2 to 3 rounds of revisions. Anything more than that requires a change in scope, which usually costs extra.
